# Methods review: Demsetz (1968)

- **Paper.** Harold Demsetz, "Why Regulate Utilities?" *Journal of Law and Economics* 11(1), April 1968,
  pp. 55–65.
- **Statistical focus.** The central claim that, with enough independent bidders for an exclusive franchise and
  costly collusion, the winning price will differ only slightly from unit cost; and the historical figures quoted
  from Behling (1938) to show that utility markets were once rivalrous, chiefly the 1902 telephone count (1,002
  cities with service, 451 of them with duplicated service).
- **Review standard.** For a theory paper, the internal consistency of the argument, how explicitly its
  assumptions are stated, and whether its predictions can be tested with observable data. The historical figures
  are judged against standard practice for reporting secondary data (source, definitions, coverage).
- **What was read.** The full text in the Portuguese translation by João Felipe Rodrigues Lanza, published in
  *MISES: Interdisciplinary Journal of Philosophy, Law and Economics* 8 (2020), which reproduces the original
  footnotes, quotations and reference list; and the bibliographic record on the University of Chicago Law School's
  Chicago Unbound repository. The English original (JSTOR 724970) was not consulted directly, so page numbers
  within the original cannot be given, and wording below is paraphrased rather than quoted.

## Methods summary

The paper is a verbal theoretical argument with no model written in equations and no estimates. It opens by
stating the textbook link between the number of producers and the competitiveness of price, quoting Samuelson
(1964) and Alchian and Allen (1964) on natural monopoly. It then argues that the link fails where it should be
strongest. Economies of scale imply that one firm will produce, but if buyers solicit bids for the right to supply
the whole market, the competitiveness of the price depends on the number of bidders and the cost of collusion,
not on the number of producers. An illustrative example (annual automobile license plates produced under scale
economies) shows the winning bid approaching unit cost. Two conditions are named: inputs available to many
potential bidders at market prices, and prohibitively costly collusion. A short informal theory of cartels follows,
arguing that a cartel paying entrants to join must eventually fail if collusion costs more than competition. The
remainder takes up the two other standard reasons for regulation, duplication of distribution facilities (recast
as an unpriced externality in the use of streets) and windfall gains under long contracts (recast as a problem of
choosing contract terms under uncertainty), and concludes that neither follows from scale economies.

## Research design

Deductive argument supported by illustrative examples and quotations. There is no empirical design, no comparison
group and no hypothesis test. The historical evidence is descriptive and secondhand.

## Sampling strategy

Not applicable to the theoretical argument. The historical figures come from Behling (1938), who in turn reports
a 1902 special report on telephone service and counts of electric and gas companies in named cities. How Behling
or the 1902 report chose or defined cities is not described in the paper.

## Sample size

Not applicable to the theory. The quoted telephone figures cover 1,051 cities with population over 4,000, of which
1,002 had telephone service. The electric and gas figures are single-city counts (New York, Chicago, Duluth,
Scranton) and are not a sample.

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Not stated. The paper deliberately sets aside marginal-cost pricing and multipart tariffs (its footnote 3), and it
sets aside durability of distribution systems, uncertainty and irrational behavior from the core example, on the
grounds that none of them is part of the natural-monopoly theory it is criticizing.

## Independent and dependent variables

Not applicable in the statistical sense. Conceptually, the outcome is the price at which the franchise is won
relative to unit cost; the determinants are the number of potential bidders (the supply elasticity of bidders) and
the cost of collusion. The paper states that these two are empirical questions that cannot be deduced from scale
economies.

## Statistical methods

None. The argument corresponds to what is now formalized as Bertrand competition for the market, or a procurement
auction, but the paper does not write that model down.

## Reported effect sizes

None estimated. The only magnitudes are the quoted historical counts listed in the table below.

## Confidence intervals

Not applicable; no estimates are reported.

## P-values, if applicable

Not applicable.

## Missing data treatment

Not applicable. For the quoted figures, the paper gives no information on cities missing from the 1902 report or on
how duplicated service was defined.

## Stated limitations

The paper is explicit that it does not argue regulatory commissions are undesirable, only that existing theory does
not justify them on grounds of monopoly price. It leaves open, as questions for a theory of collusion, when
collusion costs exceed the costs of competing, how coercive tactics and buyer cartels affect this, and how long
"eventually" is. It concedes that regulation might be preferable where pricing the use of public streets is too
costly, and that commission regulation might handle uncertainty better or worse than the market, which existing
theory cannot settle. It also states that it does not attempt to say how far legislation should replace the market.

## Interpretation notes

- The paper's figures show that early utility markets had multiple producers, which supports the narrower point
  that the supply of potential rivals was not obviously inelastic. They are not evidence on franchise bidding
  itself, which the paper proposes rather than observes.
- The core claim is a prediction about the relation between bidder numbers, collusion costs and price relative
  to cost. Its testable form is that competitively let exclusive contracts should be priced near the cost of an
  efficient operator, and more so when more independent bidders are available.
- The paper treats the problem of contract renewal briefly, arguing that predictable rents from recontracting
  costs will be bid away in the initial auction. The objection that contracts for complex, long-lived services
  are necessarily incomplete is the one Williamson (1976) developed at length; it is a disagreement about
  assumptions, not a documented error in the paper.
- No published correction or comment documenting an analytical error in the paper was found.

## Unanswered questions

- How many independent bidders are enough for the winning price to approach unit cost, and how does that number
  depend on the dispersion of bidders' costs?
- What happens at renewal, when the incumbent has learned the job and holds specialized assets?
- How should a franchise auction handle quality, which a single price bid does not pin down?
- Does the alternative actually used in practice, public ownership with competitive bidding for operations, which
  the paper mentions as an option, perform like the private franchise it has in mind?

## How the modern rerun relates

This repository does not reproduce anything in the paper, which reports no estimates. It takes the paper's
testable prediction to the National Transit Database, comparing contracted and in-house bus and paratransit service
in 2015–2025, and adds Williamson's objection as an extension. The rerun finds contracted hours about a fifth
cheaper than in-house hours within the same agency, but no sign that competitive bidding or more contractor firms
explain the gap, and no cost decline when bus service is switched to a contractor.
